How it works.

Click a step to see exactly what happens.

What it looks for
  • Attribution: “said X”, “X says”, “according to X of Y”.
  • Roles and credentials: “X, MD, a cardiologist at Y”, “Y epidemiologist X”, “X, who directs … at Y”.
  • Each person is listed with the sentence they appear in, so you can see why.
What it sets aside
  • Spokespeople, CEOs, presidents and officials. They aren't independent experts, so they start unticked.
  • Organizations that look like names (“the nonprofit Public Citizen”).
  • You can edit any name or affiliation, add someone it missed, and check up to five.
The rule
  • Search ORCID, the researcher ID registry, for the name plus the affiliation from the article.
  • Exactly one record: confirmed automatically.
  • None or several: “Identity not confirmed”, and nothing is searched.
When you pick
  • Candidate records are listed with their institutions.
  • “This is them” re-checks the record's surname against the expert before searching.
  • Why so strict? A common name matched to the wrong person is a false accusation.
Where it looks
  • Exa research-paper search for the expert's own conflict-of-interest statements.
  • Tavily search and extract for bios, board pages and disclosures.
What counts as a tie
  • A sentence about this person (by name, initials, or he/she right after) with a tie word (consulting, grants, advisory board, stock…) and an organization.
  • In “J.L. reports grants from A; M.O. reports fees from B”, only B belongs to M.O.
  • “Declares no competing interests” is never a tie. Every quote is checked against its page.
Then it sorts them. Ties to companies named in the story come first. Ties the article already disclosed are folded away. Spellings like “AstraZeneca” and “Astra Zeneca” are merged.
What it adds
  • For each tie already found, it looks up 2024–2025 payments from that company to a physician with that name.
  • The badge shows total, number of payments, payment types, city, state and NPI.
What it won't do
  • Create a tie on its own. It only supports one found in papers or bios.
  • Show a badge when several physicians with that name were paid. That's ambiguous, so it's withheld.
  • Prove identity. Open Payments lists location, not employer, so check the city.

What it can't do.

Conflict Check is built to never accuse the wrong person. That means it says less, not more, when the evidence is thin. Click a limit to see what it means in practice.

What it can miss
  • Ties that were never disclosed in any paper, bio or filing.
  • Disclosures behind paywalls, in PDFs search can't read, or on pages search doesn't index.
  • Disclosures written in unusual ways, such as a table instead of sentences.
How to read a clean result
  • “No confirmed connections” means no evidence was found. It doesn't prove there are no ties.
  • For an important story, check the expert's own recent papers and the journal's disclosure forms too.
Who is hard to confirm
  • Common names, where several researchers share the name and affiliation.
  • Clinicians, officials and industry staff, who often have no ORCID record at all.
  • Experts quoted without an affiliation, so there's nothing to match against.
What you can do
  • Fill in the affiliation in the review step, then check again.
  • Pick the right person from the candidates. The record's surname is re-checked first.
  • If no record fits, the expert stays “Identity not confirmed”. That's on purpose.
What the data covers
  • Payments from drug and device makers to US physicians, teaching hospitals and, since 2021, some other prescribers such as nurse practitioners and physician assistants.
  • Conflict Check looks at the 2024 and 2025 general payments. Companies report these, and the figures can be disputed.
  • Researchers outside the US, and payments from non-manufacturers, aren't in it.
Why it's only a badge
  • Records list name, city and state, not employer, so a name match can be a different person.
  • The badge only appears on a tie already found in papers or bios, and it's withheld when several physicians match.
Why five
  • Each expert takes several searches. Five keeps a check quick and within free API limits.
  • Most health stories quote two to four outside experts.
Checking more
  • In the review step, untick some experts and tick others, then check again.
  • Spokespeople and officials start unticked because they aren't independent experts. You can still tick them.

Every quote is checked against the page it came from. No AI model states a connection.
